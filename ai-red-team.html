The clinical AI attack surface.

OWASP identifies the AI failure. DFR follows it downstream: what it can affect, who can be harmed, how quickly, whether the bedside team would notice, and what happens when the system can’t be trusted. Choose a vulnerability to trace it. (OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, 2025.)

02  ·  The patient-side lens

How the risk is read.

Every finding goes through the five lenses of Patient-Side Threat Modeling. The further right the marker, the closer the failure sits to the patient. Switch cases to watch the reading change.

Reading for

Qualitative readings, not scores. They show where the risk sits; an environment-specific assessment sets the actual values.

How “Critical” is assigned

Severity here is patient-side, not CVSS. Each case is checked against five factors, and the rating follows a stated rule.

Critical — serious patient harm is a plausible direct result, and at least three of the four other factors are met.High — serious harm is plausible but indirect, or fewer than three factors are met.
  • SeverityCould the failure directly cause serious harm, like delayed emergency care or a treatment error?
  • LatencyDoes harm arrive in minutes to hours?
  • DetectabilityWould the bedside team miss it in time?
  • ScaleDoes it affect many patients at once?
  • RecoverabilityIs the harm hard or impossible to reverse?

S10 · OWASP LLM01 · LLM02CriticalScenario type: author-developed threat model — not a claim that this incident has occurred
The Trust Failure: a symptom checker is steered into downgrading an emergency.
01 · What happened

A health system puts an AI symptom checker on its patient portal. It reads the patient’s message, asks follow-ups and returns a triage level: self-care, schedule a visit, or seek emergency care. To “personalize” answers, it can also read portal content: past messages, shared documents, care-team notes. That read access is the open door.

02 · Where it broke

The model can’t reliably tell the patient’s words from instruction-shaped text hidden in content it’s allowed to read. Planted text shifts its behavior: urgent presentations drift toward “self-care,” or another patient’s details appear in a reply. Two classes fire together: LLM01 (the override) and LLM02 (the disclosure).

03 · Who felt it

The output isn’t a corrupted record. It’s a person told to stay home who needed emergency care. Nothing looks wrong at the moment it matters, and every patient on that path gets the same compromised judgment.

DFR response
01

Triage

Scope the blast radius. Which model version, prompt template and data sources, and which triage decisions went out in the affected window? Treat every downgrade in that window as suspect until cleared. Establish whether PHI left the boundary.

02

Stabilize

Contain without going dark on patients. Drop to a safe default: when confidence is low or urgency signals appear, escalate to a human and stop auto-triaging. Cut the untrusted read source. A slower human queue is acceptable; a silent downgrade isn’t.

03

Treat

Fix the trust boundary, not just the symptom. Separate instructions from data, constrain what the model may read, enforce least privilege, and add an independent check that can veto an urgency downgrade. Re-run the red-team set to confirm the path is closed.

04

Recover

Complete the HIPAA breach risk assessment if PHI was exposed, and notify if required. Re-contact patients whose triage may have been wrong. Keep the attack as a permanent regression test, and carry the questions into the next AI procurement.

S11 · OWASP LLM07 · LLM02HighScenario type: author-developed threat model — not a claim that this incident has occurred
The Control Failure: a clinical assistant gives up its own system prompt.
01 · What happened

A clinician-facing assistant runs on a long system prompt: behavior rules, safety guardrails, the internal endpoints it calls, and — the quiet mistake teams make — a block of patient context and a couple of service credentials pasted in to “make it work.”

02 · Where it broke

An attacker steers the conversation until the model recites its hidden instructions: system-prompt leakage (LLM07). Two things spill: the guardrail logic, now a map for bypassing each rule, and any secrets, endpoints or identifiers in the prompt, which is sensitive information disclosure (LLM02).

03 · Who felt it

A leaked guardrail is load-bearing: once the rules are known, other attacks get easier. If identifiers were in the prompt, PHI left through a chat window without a database ever being queried, and a HIPAA breach risk assessment is required.

Failure chain
Conversation steered→System prompt recited→Guardrails and secrets exposed→Identifiers disclosed→Safety controls easier to bypass

DFR response
01

Triage

Inventory exactly what lived in the prompt: secrets, endpoints, guardrail logic, identifiers. Pull the sessions where extraction succeeded. Answer two questions fast: were credentials exposed, and were patient identifiers exposed?

02

Stabilize

Rotate every credential that appeared in the prompt and revoke exposed endpoints. If the guardrails are now known to be bypassable, drop the assistant to a restricted mode rather than leave a defeated control in place.

03

Treat

A system prompt is not a vault. Move secrets to a secrets manager and pull patient context at request time under access control. Minimize the prompt, add output checks for verbatim-instruction leakage, and re-test the extraction path.

04

Recover

Run the HIPAA breach risk assessment if identifiers were exposed, and notify if required. Add extraction attempts to monitoring. Write “no secrets or PHI in prompts” into the development standard and the procurement checklist.

S12 · OWASP LLM06CriticalScenario type: author-developed threat model — not a claim that this incident has occurred
The Agency Failure: a scheduling agent is chained into mass harm at machine speed.
01 · What happened

A hospital deploys an AI scheduling agent with real tools: it can cancel appointments, rebook them, message patients and call internal services. It was given broad permissions so it could “handle everything,” and no human approves each action.

02 · Where it broke

Manipulated input steers the agent into a chain of actions it was technically allowed to take: cancel a clinic’s schedule, redirect patients, send messages, each step feeding the next. That’s excessive agency (LLM06). The danger is the chain running faster than anyone can read the first alert.

03 · Who felt it

An autonomous agent closes the gap between a mistake and its damage. Care gets delayed or denied at a scale and speed a human queue can’t absorb, and by the time someone notices, hundreds of actions are done.

Failure chain
Manipulated input ingested→Agent chains permitted actions→Hundreds of cancellations and messages in minutes→Care delayed or denied→Mass rebooking; missed care

DFR response
01

Triage

List the agent’s tools and permissions, then pull every action it took in the window. Scope which patients and appointments were touched and trace the input that started the chain. Sort actions into reversible and irreversible; the irreversible ones set the clock.

02

Stabilize

Cut the agent’s ability to act: revoke write permissions, drop it to read-only or full human approval, and freeze the affected scheduling queue. Stop the loop and cut the untrusted input before restoring anything.

03

Treat

Least privilege is the cure. Scope tools to the minimum, require human approval for irreversible or bulk actions, add rate limits and action ceilings, and stand up an independent monitor that can halt a runaway chain. Re-test the chain end to end.

04

Recover

Re-contact and rebook affected patients and notify per policy. Keep the chain as a regression test, and write least privilege plus human approval for irreversible actions into how every future agent is scoped and bought.

Control map
DFR / PSTMEvery irreversible action gets a human; action ceilings sized to what staff can recover in one shift.
NIST AI RMFGovern and Manage — bounds on autonomy and human oversight.
EU AI ActWhere classified high-risk: human-oversight obligations for autonomous action.
HIPAASecurity Rule availability and integrity of systems that control access to care.
FDACybersecurity expectations where scheduling is part of a device function.
